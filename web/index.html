A portfolio project

Second Ear

A program that works out where sounds come from, and what it loses when it has only one ear.

1. The problem 2. The showcase 3. How it works

1 · The problem

Which way did that come from?

I'm deaf in my left ear. I can hear almost everything, but I often can't tell where a sound is coming from: when someone calls my name in a busy room, I can turn the wrong way. So I built a model that listens to a room with two ears, then with only my right one, to show what that difference looks like.

3.7° average error finding a sound in front, with two ears
22.4° the same, with one ear (single-sided deafness)

A degree is 1/360 of a full turn. Smaller is better. Source: Aruffo et al. 2025, Audiology Research 15(4):85, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12286218.

Drag the sound around the head

Why is one ear so limiting? Sound from the right reaches your right ear a tiny moment before the left. Your brain uses that gap to point. Drag the dot and watch the gap.

  • The sound (drag it, or use the arrow keys)
  • Its front/back mirror twin: gives the same delay
  • Ear that is working
  • Ear that is switched off

The click uses your browser's 3D-sound mode (HRTF, a model of how a head bends sound). Headphones needed. The time gap uses the Woodworth formula for a head of radius 8.75 cm and a sound speed of 343 m/s.

3 · How it works

From sound to a map

Think of a doctor reading an X-ray. The program cannot "listen" the way we do, so we first turn sound into a picture, then let a pattern-finding program read the picture.

Every tenth of a second it answers: for each direction around the listener, how likely is it that a sound comes from there? The answers are painted as a heat map, bright where it thinks a sound is.

  • Bright patch: the model thinks a sound is in that direction
  • Dark purple to pale yellow: quiet to loud (spectrogram and floor)

What I did, in five pieces

1. Training scenes from real ear measurements

Practice sound scenes were built by mixing free sound clips (FSD50K) through measured "ear filters" called HRIRs (head-related impulse responses: how a real head and ear change a sound that arrives from each direction). The filters come from two public sets, HUTUBS and SADIE II, so no single person's ears are learned. 600 such scenes were made for training.

These scenes are rendered by computer, not recorded in real rooms.

2. The heat-map model

It reuses the front end of the published DCASE 2023 contest baseline, then draws a map of every direction (in 5-degree steps) for each of 13 sound types, 10 times a second. A map can show two sounds of the same type at once, and it feeds the 3D viewer directly.

3. Fair testing

The cut-off a bright spot must pass to count as a guess was chosen on 100 separate practice scenes, once per model, and never on the test scenes. Tuning it on the test scenes would be cheating. Every method is scored by the same code.

4. Sudden loss vs an adapted listener

Sudden loss: the two-ear model with one ear silenced, like losing an ear yesterday. Adapted: a model trained on one ear from the start, like someone who has lived with it for years. In the last run, sudden loss collapsed the model's fair score by more than tenfold, and the adapted models won back about half (fair score: two ears 3.4%, sudden loss 0.1 to 0.3%, adapted 1.7 to 1.8%)updated soon.

Adapted models also made 4 to 6 guesses per real sound, which flatters them. People with one ear still beat every model here.

5. Bugs found and fixed

  • The first heat-map run fired on every frame because the last layer started at 50% "sound here". Starting it near zero fixed it, and the model then memorised one clip perfectly, the standard sanity check.
  • The heat-map model under-detects because its cut-off was fixed before testing; choosing it on held-out scenes is what the one-ear tests do instead.
  • Converting the real recordings to two ears loses much of the delay between the ears, so the two-ear data is rendered from head filters instead.

Model card

  • Intended use: research, learning and a portfolio project: exploring how much direction information one ear loses. Not a medical device, not a hearing aid, not for safety decisions.
  • Data: STARSS23 (real recordings from 16 rooms in Finland and Japan) for the first models; rendered two-ear scenes from FSD50K clips with HUTUBS and SADIE II head filters for the one-ear experiment.
  • Where it works so far: official contest score (F20) 9.7%updated soon, average miss 25.8° when it fires, but it finds only 13.5% of soundsupdated soon.
  • Limits: it misses many real sounds; echoes pull guesses off course; overlapping sounds blur; with one ear, front and back are easily confused (so the model draws a map, not one arrow). Only 13 sound types, only a few head shapes, and the 3D floor waves are a simple simulation, not real acoustics.
