A portfolio project

Second Ear

A program that works out where sounds come from, and what it loses when it has only one ear.

1. The problem 2. The showcase 3. How it works

1 · The problem

Which way did that come from?

I'm deaf in my left ear. I can hear almost everything, but I often can't tell where a sound is coming from: when someone calls my name in a busy room, I can turn the wrong way. So I built a model that listens to a room with two ears, then with only my right one, to show what that difference looks like.

3.7° average error finding a sound in front, with two ears
22.4° the same, with one ear (single-sided deafness)

A degree is 1/360 of a full turn. Smaller is better. Source: Aruffo et al. 2025, Audiology Research 15(4):85, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12286218.

How well does it do?

3 · How it works

From two ears to a guess in the café

A computer cannot "listen" the way we do. So the sound is first turned into a picture, and a pattern finding program reads the picture.

Ten times a second the program answers one question for every direction around the listener, in 5 degree steps: how likely is it that a sound comes from there? The answers are painted as a heat map (a picture where bright means "yes, probably"). Then the brightest spots become guesses.

In the café overview, each guess is a puck on a ring around the listener, and each real sound is a ring marker. If the puck is within 20 degrees of the true spot, it counts as a hit.

  • Bright patch: the model thinks a sound is in that direction
  • Ring marker: where the sound really is

What I did

Six steps, in the order they happened. "Hit score" (called F20 in the project files) is the share of guesses and real sounds that match within 20 degrees. Higher is better, and 100% is perfect.

  1. Build practice scenes on the computer

    I mixed free sound clips (from a library called FSD50K) through measured head filters (two public sets named HUTUBS and SADIE II). A head filter copies how a real head and ear bend a sound that comes from one direction. The result is left ear and right ear audio. I made 4,000 scenes to practice on and 300 to test on, and the test scenes use head filters the model never practiced with.

    These scenes are made by computer, not recorded in real rooms.

  2. Teach a heat map model

    A neural network (a program that learns from examples). It reuses the front end of the published DCASE 2023 contest baseline (DCASE stands for Detection and Classification of Acoustic Scenes and Events, a yearly sound contest) and adds a part that draws the direction map. One map shows every sound at once, whatever its type.

  3. Choose the cut-off fairly

    A bright spot only becomes a guess if it passes a cut-off. I picked each model's cut-off on separate scenes, never on the test scenes, because tuning on the test would be cheating (two ears 0.30, one ear 0.20). An earlier model with a fixed cut-off of 0.5 missed most sounds: it found 13.5% of them, against 35.7% for the contest baseline.

  4. Take one ear away

    On the 300 test scenes, the two ear model gets a hit score of 54%. Silence its left ear at test time and it falls to 4.6%, like losing an ear yesterday. Models trained with one ear from the start reach 13% to 14%, like someone who has lived with it for years.

    Those one ear models made about 2.4 to 2.5 guesses for each real sound, which flatters their score. People with one ear still beat every model here.

  5. Try real recordings

    LOCATA is a public set of real recordings made with hearing aid microphones on a dummy head (46 test recordings). Asked only "left or right?", the two ear model scored 0.479 where random guessing scores 0.234. Either single ear scored 0.207 or 0.216, which is no better than chance. After more training on 12 of the 18 practice recordings, the two ear hit score on real audio rose from 0.099 to 0.543.

    That is one room and one dummy head, so it means "tuned for this room", not "works anywhere".

  6. Put it in the café

    I ran two of these models on the same 30 second café clip and drew their guesses in the 3D scene above. The box below says how they did.

In plain words: with two ears the model put about half of the real sounds (50%) within 20 degrees. With only my ear it found about one in nine (11%) and scattered many more wrong guesses (1,482 guesses, against 769 for two ears). The average miss was 33 degrees with two ears and 48 degrees with one.

Model card

Heat map model, short version

What it is
The encoder (front end) of the DCASE 2023 baseline plus a map head. Every 0.1 second it draws a grid of directions in 5 degree steps. The café uses the "where only" versions, which ignore what kind of sound it is and only say where.
Trained on
First models: STARSS23 (Sony-TAu Realistic Spatial Soundscapes 2023), real recordings in rooms in Finland and Japan. Ear models: 4,000 computer made scenes from FSD50K clips with HUTUBS and SADIE II head filters. Fine tuning: 12 of the 18 LOCATA practice recordings.
Tested on
300 computer made scenes with head filters never seen in training, then 46 real LOCATA recordings.
Where it works
Two ears, simulated test: hit score 54%, average miss 24.9 degrees, about 1.09 guesses per real sound. When only one sound plays, the miss for sounds in front drops to 11.7 degrees.
Where it fails
It misses many real sounds. Echoes pull guesses off course and overlapping sounds blur. With one ear, front and back are easily confused, which is why it draws a map and not one arrow. On real recordings it gets left and right but not front and back. Harder practice scenes with real rooms and café noise did not beat the earlier models. The original model knows 13 sound types and only the rooms in Finland and Japan. Only a few head shapes were used, and the floor waves in the advanced view are a simple simulation, not real acoustics.
Intended use
Research, learning and a portfolio project: finding out how much direction information one ear loses. Not a medical device, not a hearing aid, not for safety decisions.
